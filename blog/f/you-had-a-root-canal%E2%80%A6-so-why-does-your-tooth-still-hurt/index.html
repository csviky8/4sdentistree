You Had a Root Canal… So Why Does Your Tooth Still Hurt?

9 October 2026

You finally had the root canal.

The infection has been treated. The nerve inside the tooth has been removed. You were expecting the pain to disappear.

But a day or two later, you bite down and feel a dull ache.

Or the tooth feels tender when you touch it.

And then comes the worrying thought:

“Did my root canal fail?”

If you are experiencing pain after root canal treatment, you are not necessarily dealing with a failed treatment.

Some soreness or tenderness for a few days can be a normal part of healing, particularly if the tooth was infected or painful before treatment. Mild soreness or tenderness for a few days is common and should generally improve as the surrounding tissues heal.

Research also shows that post-treatment pain is not unusual. A systematic review found that pain prevalence falls substantially after root canal treatment, with pain levels generally dropping from the first day through the first week.

So the important question isn't simply:

“Does it hurt?”

It is:

“What kind of pain am I having, and is it getting better or worse?”

Is Pain After Root Canal Treatment Normal?

Mild discomfort can be normal.

Even though the infected pulp has been removed, the tissues surrounding the root of the tooth are still present. If these tissues were irritated by the original infection or by the treatment itself, they may need some time to settle.

You may experience:

Mild soreness

Tenderness when biting

A feeling that the tooth is slightly different from the surrounding teeth

Mild sensitivity around the treated area

Some jaw soreness from keeping your mouth open during the procedure

These symptoms can occur for a few days following treatment and usually improve during the healing period.

One systematic review found that average post-treatment pain decreased substantially within the first 24 hours and was minimal for most patients by one week.

So if your tooth is a little sore but getting better each day, that is generally more reassuring than pain that is becoming progressively worse.

Why Does My Tooth Still Hurt After a Root Canal?

There isn't just one explanation.

Here are some of the most common possibilities.

1. The Area Around the Root Is Still Healing

This is one of the simplest explanations.

A root canal treats the inside of the tooth, but the tissues surrounding the root may have been inflamed before treatment.

If there was a significant infection or abscess before the root canal, those tissues don't instantly return to normal the moment treatment is completed.

Think of it like treating an infection:

The cause may have been treated, but the surrounding tissues still need time to recover.

This is why some patients experience tenderness when biting for several days after treatment.

What you can do

Follow the medication and aftercare instructions given by your dentist or endodontist.

Unless you have been specifically advised otherwise, avoid chewing hard foods on the treated tooth while it is recovering or until it has been permanently restored.

2. Your Tooth Was Already Very Inflamed or Infected

A tooth that was severely painful before the root canal may take longer to settle than a tooth that had little or no pre-treatment pain.

Research has consistently shown that postoperative pain can occur even after appropriately performed root canal treatment.

So don't judge the success of the treatment based solely on how you feel in the first 24–48 hours.

3. Your Bite May Feel Different

Sometimes patients say:

“The root canal tooth hurts every time I bite.”

One possibility is that the tooth is taking more biting force than it should because the bite is slightly uneven after treatment or restoration.

Contact the dentist if your bite feels unevenafter root canal treatment.

This is worth checking rather than simply tolerating the discomfort.

What to do

Tell your dentist exactly what you feel:

“The tooth hurts when I bite down, and it feels like this tooth touches first.”

Your dentist can examine the bite and determine whether the restoration or another factor needs attention.

4. The Temporary Filling or Crown May Be a Problem

Depending on how your treatment was completed, you may have a temporary filling or temporary crown before the final restoration.

If it becomes loose or comes out, the tooth needs to be assessed.

A root canal is not necessarily the final step.

The treated tooth often needs a permanent restoration to protect it from fracture and restore its function. The timing and type of restoration depend on the tooth and the amount of remaining tooth structure.

5. The Original Problem May Not Be Completely Resolved

This is where persistent or worsening pain deserves proper evaluation.

A root canal can occasionally require additional treatment.

Possible reasons include:

Complex or unusual root canal anatomy

A canal that was not fully treated

Persistent or recurrent infection

A breakdown of the restoration or seal

New decay

A crack or fracture

New infection developing later

An endodontically treated tooth can occasionally fail to heal or can develop disease months or years later, and in some cases retreatment or endodontic surgery can save the tooth.

This does not mean that persistent pain automatically means the root canal was done incorrectly.

It means the tooth needs to be diagnosed rather than guessed about.

6. The Pain May Not Actually Be Coming From the Root Canal Tooth

This is something patients often don't realize.

Pain felt in a particular area does not always identify its exact source.

For example, pain may sometimes be related to:

A neighboring tooth

A cracked tooth

Bite or clenching forces

Sinus-related pain in some upper teeth

Other non-tooth-related sources of facial pain

Some tooth-region pain can have nonodontogenic causes, which is one reason persistent pain requires proper examination rather than automatically assuming the root canal has failed.

This is also why your dentist may need to examine more than just the tooth that had the root canal.

Contact your dentist or endodontist if you experience:

🚩 Severe pain or pressure

If the pain is severe, persistent or continues beyond the expected early healing period, it should be evaluated. Contact an endodontist for severe pain or pressure lasting more than a few days.

🚩 Increasing pain instead of decreasing pain

Your pain should generally trend toward improvement.

If you were feeling better and then suddenly become significantly worse, don't simply wait it out.

🚩 Swelling

Visible swelling inside or outside the mouth is a reason to contact your dentist promptly.

🚩 The symptoms you had before treatment return

If the same severe toothache, swelling or other symptoms that led to the root canal come back, the tooth needs reassessment.

🚩 Your bite feels uneven

If the tooth feels like it is hitting first or is painful every time you bite, tell your dentist.

🚩 Your temporary filling or crown comes out

Don't ignore it. Contact your dentist so the tooth can be protected appropriately.

What Can You Do for Pain After Root Canal Treatment?

Your first step should be to follow the specific instructions given by your dentist or endodontist.

Depending on your individual situation, your dentist may recommend an appropriate over-the-counter pain reliever or prescribe medication.

Don't take medication simply because someone else had the same procedure. Your medical history, other medicines and individual circumstances matter.

You can also:

Avoid chewing hard foods on the treated tooth.

Follow your prescribed medication schedule.

Maintain normal brushing and flossing, while being gentle around the area.

Attend your scheduled follow-up.

Complete the permanent restoration when advised.

And importantly:

Don't repeatedly press, tap or bite on the tooth just to “check” whether it still hurts.

If the area is healing, repeatedly testing it can simply irritate it.

How Long Should Pain Last After a Root Canal?

There isn't one exact number of days that applies to every patient.

Mild discomfort commonly lasts for a few days, with most patients improving within approximately 3–5 days.

Research also shows that pain tends to decrease substantially during the first week.

So rather than focusing only on the number of days, ask:

Is the pain getting better?

A mild ache that is steadily improving is generally more reassuring than pain that is becoming stronger, returning after it had improved, or occurring with swelling.

Can a Root Canal Tooth Hurt Months or Years Later?

It can happen, although it is not the expected course.

. Possible reasons include new decay, trauma, restoration problems or persistent/recurrent infection. Some teeth can be managed with retreatment or endodontic surgery.

Persistent tooth pain after endodontic treatment is relatively uncommon overall. A systematic review estimated persistent pain at six months or longer at around 5.3%, although the studies were heterogeneous and the causes of pain varied.

So long-term pain deserves investigation—but it doesn't automatically mean the tooth needs to be extracted.

A Simple Rule to Remember

After a root canal, think about three things:

Mild + improving = usually monitor

Some soreness or tenderness for a few days can be part of normal healing.

Severe or worsening = call

Increasing pain, significant pressure or swelling deserves professional assessment.

Persistent or returning = investigate

If the pain isn't settling, or the symptoms you had before treatment return, don't suffer silently.

Your dentist can determine whether the tooth is healing normally, whether the bite or restoration needs attention, or whether further endodontic evaluation is required.

Frequently Asked Questions About Pain After Root Canal Treatment

1. Is pain after root canal treatment normal?

Mild soreness or tenderness for a few days can be normal, especially if the tooth was infected or painful before treatment. The discomfort should generally improve rather than progressively worsen.

2. How long should pain last after a root canal?

Many patients experience mild discomfort for a few days, and symptoms commonly improve within approximately 3–5 days. Persistent or increasing pain should be assessed by your dentist or endodontist.

3. Why does my tooth still hurt after a root canal?

The surrounding tissues may still be healing, particularly if there was significant inflammation or infection before treatment. Other possibilities include an uneven bite, restoration-related problems, persistent infection, a crack or pain coming from another source.

4. Is it normal for a root canal tooth to hurt when biting?

Some tenderness when biting can occur during early healing. However, if the tooth feels like it is hitting first, the pain is severe, or the biting pain persists or worsens, contact your dentist for an assessment.

5. Can a root canal fail if the tooth still hurts?

Pain immediately after treatment does not mean the root canal has failed. However, persistent or returning pain, swelling or other symptoms may require further examination to determine whether additional treatment is needed.

6. What should I do if my root canal tooth hurts after one week?

If pain is still present after a week, particularly if it is not steadily improving, contact your dentist or endodontist. The tooth may simply need monitoring, but persistent symptoms deserve an examination rather than self-diagnosis.

7. Can I take painkillers after a root canal?

Your dentist may recommend an appropriate pain medication based on your individual circumstances. Follow the medication instructions provided to you rather than taking someone else's prescription or exceeding the recommended dose.

8. When should I call my dentist after a root canal?

Call your dentist if you have severe or increasing pain, significant pressure, swelling, an uneven bite, a temporary filling or crown that comes out, or a return of the symptoms you experienced before treatment.

9. Does swelling after a root canal mean there is an infection?

Swelling should not simply be assumed to be normal. Visible swelling inside or outside the mouth after treatment is a reason to contact your dentist or endodontist for assessment.

10. Should I wait for root canal pain to go away on its own?

If the pain is mild and clearly improving, monitoring it while following your dentist's aftercare instructions may be appropriate. If it is severe, worsening, associated with swelling, or persistent without improvement, contact your dentist rather than suffering silently.
